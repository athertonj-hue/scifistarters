Science Curriculum Coverage Map

The long-term aim is to build a balanced library of film starters across the whole science curriculum, not simply collect memorable scenes. This first framework uses the science programmes of study for England (Key Stages 1–4) as its starting point because the current library already uses KS1–KS4 age guidance.

Biology

  • Cells, organisation and body systems
  • Living things, habitats and ecology
  • Plants, photosynthesis and bioenergetics
  • Inheritance, variation, evolution and genetics
  • Health, disease and homeostasis

Current examples: plants and habitats (WALL-E); DNA and fossils (Jurassic Park); genetic screening (Gattaca).

Chemistry

  • Particles, states of matter and separation
  • Atoms, elements, compounds and the periodic table
  • Bonding, structure and material properties
  • Chemical changes, equations and quantitative chemistry
  • Energy changes, rates, organic chemistry and resources

Current examples: rates of reaction (The Empire Strikes Back; Gremlins); hydrazine and combustion (The Martian); fusion versus chemical reactions (Spider-Man 2).

Physics

  • Forces, motion and pressure
  • Energy stores, transfers and resources
  • Electricity, circuits and electrical safety
  • Waves, light and sound
  • Magnetism, electromagnets and electromagnetism
  • Particle model and atomic physics

Current examples: forces and robotics (Big Hero 6); sound in space (Star Wars); electromagnets (You Only Live Twice); lasers and electrical safety (Goldfinger).

Earth & Space

  • Earth structure, rocks and the atmosphere
  • Space physics, the Solar System and the universe
  • Climate, environmental change and natural resources

Current examples: Earth's internal structure and magnetic field (The Core); human impact and planetary environment (WALL-E).

Working Scientifically

  • Asking questions and developing hypotheses
  • Planning investigations and identifying variables
  • Using apparatus, measurement and safe procedures
  • Recording, presenting and analysing evidence
  • Evaluating methods, uncertainty and conclusions
  • Considering models, scientific explanations and ethics

Across the library: use each clip to evaluate evidence, test a model, identify an unrealistic claim or design a follow-up investigation.

Coverage status: framework established; full audit still in progress. The examples above show topics already represented in the current library. They do not mean every listed curriculum area is covered adequately. The next development stage is to map each individual clip to precise programme-of-study statements and identify gaps by age/key stage and discipline. The framework currently starts with England; international curriculum cross-mapping can follow.

Reference framework: Department for Education – National curriculum in England: science programmes of study.

Consistent Clip Timestamp Standard

All clip entries use the same time format so teachers can locate scenes quickly and compare entries consistently.

Format

HH:MM:SS–HH:MM:SS

Use a start and end time for the shortest useful teaching extract, not the whole sequence where a shorter moment will do.

Verification

Record the film edition or release used to check the timecode (for example, a named Blu-ray, DVD or streaming edition). Intro logos, opening credits, PAL/NTSC versions and platform edits can shift timings.

Honest status labels

Verified: checked against a named edition.

Provisional: indicative timing; teacher should check their copy.

To locate: scene identified, exact in/out points not yet checked.

Current library status: timings already present in entries are retained, but are treated as provisional until checked against a named edition. Entries without precise timings are marked for location rather than given guessed timecodes. This keeps the presentation consistent without implying that an unverified time is exact.
   

From Classroom Research to Global Practice

   

SciFiStarters.org translates findings from MA Science Education research conducted by Justin Atherton and Dr Heather King at King’s College London, published in the Association for Science Education’s School Science Review (Issue 398): “The use of science fiction clips as lesson starter activities”.

   

Classroom trials showed that short film clips spark high engagement, but only succeed when teachers frame them with targeted questioning to stop students accepting plausible-looking Hollywood pseudo-science. Every entry here is designed to remove teacher prep time while protecting scientific accuracy.
